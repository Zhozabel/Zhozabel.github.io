Oakridge was one of those small, cozy towns where the two-story houses with steep-pitched tile roofs looked as though
they had been painted on a Christmas postcard. Everyone knew everyone by sight, neighbors greeted each other over low
wooden fences, and life moved at such a calm, predictable pace that you walked into every new day with absolute
certainty: no unpleasant surprises lay ahead.

Isabella had been used to this quiet unhurriedness since childhood.

A cold autumn draft, sneaking through the warped window frame, woke the girl up completely. The relentless, dreary howling of the wind outside was enough
to drive anyone up the wall.

“Who on earth installed these windows? I ought to break his hands!” Isabella cursed under her breath as she lazily sat
up in bed, rubbing her sleepy eyes.

The icy air sent a wave of goosebumps down her spine, instantly whisking away the last remnants of sleep. She had to
hurry — school wouldn't wait.

Her bag, stuffed to the brim with notebooks, squatted right at the foot of the chair, as if deliberately waiting for its
victim. Shivering and teeth chattering from the cold, Isabella quickly shed her pajamas and began pulling on her tights,
trying to balance on one foot.

Naturally, the moment she took a step to the side, her foot caught in the bag’s strap. The heavy backpack hit the floor
with a loud thud and a clatter of notebooks, and Isabella tumbled right after it with a quiet squeak, hitting her knee
hard against the cold floorboards.

“Dammit…” she hissed, wincing in frustration as she struggled back to her feet.

Her mood, which had already been hanging by a thread, plummeted to rock bottom. Hastily tugging up her tights, she threw
on a white blouse, her favorite faded jeans, and a soft pink vest, shooting an absolute death glare at the offending
bag.

Downstairs, the kitchen smelled of cinnamon and toasted bread. A cup of hot tea sat on the table, thoughtfully covered
with a saucer to keep it from cooling down. A faint smile touched Isabella’s lips at the prospect of the warming drink.
Mrs. Rodriguez brought a stack of brown paper lunch bags from the living room and, opening one, carefully slipped a
sandwich inside.

“Good morning, sweetie,” her mother said, looking at her warmly.

“Morning, Mom,” Isabella replied, taking a eager, lifesaving sip of the warm tea.

Outside, the sharp honk of the school bus cut through the air. Isabella bolted from her chair, grabbing her bag and
jacket on the fly, and burst out onto the porch.

“Isabella! You forgot your math textbook!” her mother called out after her, appearing in the doorway with the heavy
hardcover in hand.

“I won't be needing it today!” Isabella called back, vaulting into the open doors of the bus on the run.

It was mid-October. The gloomy, drizzly weather served as a honest warning: November was creeping up. Isabella genuinely
hated this time of year. The biting slush and heavy, leaden sky seemed to stomp any attempt at a good mood right into
the mud. During this season, even the most cheerful residents of Oakridge looked somber and distant. All anyone could do
was endure the autumn blues and wait for the first snow, which usually brought everyone a second wind.

As for math, Isabella truly had no use for the textbook. Their elderly teacher, Mrs. Turner, had been on sick leave for
two weeks now — the poor woman hadn't survived the damp October drafts and had come down with a severe case of pneumonia.
And though nobody in class particularly liked math, everyone was genuinely worried about Mrs. Turner's health. Rumors
were already circulating that the principal was desperately searching for a substitute for the senior classes, but for
now, they simply spent those periods hanging out in the classroom or the library.

Feeling the oppressive autumn gloom trying to creep back in, Isabella pulled her headphones from her pocket, untangled
the cord, and leaned back into her seat, turning on her favorite playlist.

Sinking into her own world didn't last long, however. A couple of minutes later, a light, gentle tap on her shoulder
pulled her back to reality.

Isabella pulled out an earbud and turned around. Theodore Moore was standing beside her.

They had known each other since childhood — their mothers had been friends since high school, so Isabella was used to
seeing Theodore and his younger brother, Adrian, more often than her own cousins.

“Hey, Izzy! Morning!” Theo offered a wide grin, running a hand through his already messy red hair. “Mind if I sit?” He
nodded toward the empty seat beside her.

“Of course not,” Isabella smiled back, feeling a burning wave of heat instantly flush her cheeks.

With a familiar, awkward gesture, she tucked a strand of hair behind her ear and glued her gaze to her knees, not daring
to look him in the eye. Her heart pounded somewhere up in her throat, and a familiar, tingling flutter danced across her
skin. It was her body's standard reaction to Theodore Moore — a reaction she had been trying to get used to for the past
three years.

Isabella genuinely couldn't comprehend how Theo still hadn't guessed her feelings. To him, she had always just been the
neighborhood girl, a childhood pal. To her, every clumsy move felt like a neon sign broadcasting her secret within a
five-meter radius: the silly fidgeting, her burning face, her trembling fingers… It all felt painfully obvious. But
Theo, having grown up side-by-side with her, simply didn't know how to look at her any other way.

Theo flopped happily onto the soft, brown faux-leather seat and turned to face her. At that exact moment, another gust
of rain slammed against the window with a icy, rattling sound.

“You're quiet today,” Theo remarked, studying her face. Noticing how drearily Isabella was watching the raindrops streak
down the glass, he nudged her elbow with a grin: “Hey, fortune-teller. Which of these two drops do you think hits the
bottom first? Right or left?”

Isabella shifted her gaze to the glass, catching his playful tone:

“Right,” she declared confidently, a smile breaking through despite herself.

“Wrong! Left,” Theo rubbed his hands together eagerly. “Tell you what: if your drop wins, I’ll buy you a coffee and that
chocolate croissant you like during lunch break. And if mine wins…” He paused dramatically, “…I’ll still buy you the
coffee and croissant.”

Isabella couldn't help letting out a soft laugh. The sticky gloom of the autumn morning shattered instantly.

“Hey, that’s no fair,” she stammered slightly, feeling the knot of nervousness tighten in her throat again. “If yours
wins… I’ll invite you over for dinner. Mom's making chicken quesadillas tonight. And… if you… I mean, if you and Adrian
want to, you can come over. Mom’s always happy to have you guys.”

Theo smirked triumphantly and pointed a finger at the glass:

“Look, look! Your drop just overtook mine! So coffee's on me.”

Isabella let out a quiet sigh of relief — it seemed Theo hadn't noticed her stumbling. But inside, she was burning with
frustration at herself.

“Idiot, idiot, what an absolute idiot!” her mind screamed. “Why did I have to drag Adrian into this?! If you wanted to
invite Theo, just invite Theo! What does Adrian have to do with any of this?!”

From pure anger at her own cowardice, Isabella clenched her fists so tight her nails bit painfully into her palms.
Whenever Theo was around, her confidence completely evaporated, leaving behind nothing but a jumble of nervous babble.

Just then, the bus lurched to a sudden stop in the school parking lot, swaying all the passengers forward. The sudden
flurry of movement, chatter, and familiar school noise forced Isabella out of her self-flagellation.

They stepped out of the bus into the pouring rain and walked side by side along the paved path toward the tall school
doors — the very same path hundreds of students tread day in and day out.

They had to hurry. There were only five minutes left before the bell.
